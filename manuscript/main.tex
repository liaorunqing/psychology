\title[Early and recent personal baselines]{Early versus recent personal baselines in repeated psychological self-reports: a cross-dataset prediction study}

\author[1]{\fnm{Runqing} \sur{Liao}}\email{202411055222@stu.hstc.edu.cn}
\author[2]{\fnm{Jiabao} \sur{Zhao}}\email{zjb200101011@163.com}

\abstract{\textbf{Background:} Repeated assessments often compare a person's current state with a personal baseline established near the beginning of monitoring. That comparison assumes that the early baseline remains representative. We examined whether later self-reports were better understood from an equally sized recent history and whether any advantage could be explained by ordinary temporal persistence.

\textbf{Methods:} We conducted secondary analyses of three development datasets---Dejonckheere/openESM ($n=100$), the College Experience Study ($n=105$), and Marian/openESM ($n=145$)---and a protocol-locked external extension in the Corona Health app cohort (PHQ-9, $n=324$). For each report after calibration, we compared two personal references. The early reference was the mean of the first eight reports; the recent reference was the mean of the eight reports immediately preceding the target. Participants were weighted equally. Stationary-process simulations, alternative calibration origins, ordinal-score checks, and simpler updating rules were used to probe competing explanations.

\textbf{Results:} In the three development datasets, recent histories reduced mean absolute error by 9.97\%--29.80\% across eight outcomes when each participant received equal weight. In the external extension, PHQ-9 error fell from 2.20 to 1.83 points, a 16.60\% reduction (95\% CI 13.18\% to 20.11\%). This extension was protocol-locked rather than preregistered and used a one-week recall instruction. Its direction was unchanged in the 30-day gap and ordinal-score analyses, and the observed advantage exceeded the fitted Gaussian AR(1) distribution. Re-anchoring preserved advantages for stress, PHQ-2, and depressed mood, with inconclusive evidence for sadness. Average advantages increased with the age of the original baseline, but person-specific gradients did not generalise across time blocks.

\textbf{Conclusions:} A personal baseline can remain individualized while becoming less representative of a person's current reports. Updating the reference improved prediction across different assessment timescales, but the size and interpretation of that improvement depended on the outcome and analytic assumptions. Prospective monitoring studies should treat the choice and age of a personal baseline as part of the measurement design.}

\section{Background}

When psychological states are measured repeatedly, the most intuitive comparison is often with the same person's earlier reports. A sadness score of 40 may signal a marked change for someone who usually reports little sadness, but an ordinary day for someone whose scores are typically higher. Personal baselines make this distinction possible. They allow researchers to interpret a current report in the context of the person who provided it rather than against a population average alone \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3}.

This strength also creates a subtler problem. A personal baseline is individual, but it is tied to a particular period. The first days of an experience-sampling study may capture novelty or entry-related behaviour. For a student followed over several years, they may reflect a different academic term, social setting, or phase of symptoms. A fixed early reference can remain person-specific while becoming less representative of the person's current context. This possibility matters whenever repeated assessments are used to identify change, tailor feedback, or forecast later states.

Longitudinal methods use personal history in different ways. A fixed mean emphasises continuity, a last observation emphasises immediate carry-over, and time-series or multilevel models combine information across timescales \cite{ref2,ref5,ref7}. Smartphone and wearable studies show that the value of person-specific modelling varies across people and outcomes \cite{ref8,ref9,ref10}. Recent work treats a personal baseline as a forecasting rule whose usefulness depends on the task, context, and updating scheme \cite{ref20}. It remains unclear whether an early personal reference is as informative as an equally simple recent reference.

Related research has shown that predictive value changes with the temporal window and that weak validation can exaggerate gains from complex mobile-health models \cite{ref18,ref19}. Schat et al. used the same Dejonckheere source data to study exponentially weighted monitoring with limited baseline data, whereas Choi et al. used the same CES source to model behavioural-routine variability and affective states \cite{ref21,ref23}. Those studies addressed monitoring algorithms and behavioural predictors, respectively. Neither isolated the timing of self-report history by comparing equally sized early and recent histories with the same estimator, which is the narrower question examined here.

That comparison is not straightforward. Recent reports are usually more correlated with the next report, even when the underlying process is stable. Better prediction from a recent reference need not indicate enduring psychological change. We held the amount and form of information constant by constructing both references as means of eight earlier reports. We then compared the observed advantage with advantages expected under stationary temporal processes. This design separates the timing of personal information from its amount and form.

We first examined the comparison across three datasets with markedly different timescales. One followed PHQ-2 symptoms in university students over several years \cite{ref11}. Two experience-sampling datasets measured affect, stress, anhedonia, and depressed mood several times per day over two or three weeks \cite{ref12,ref13}. We then locked an analysis protocol before applying the same comparison to repeated PHQ-9 scores from the independently sourced Corona Health smartphone-app dataset \cite{ref24,ref28}. We expected recent histories to predict subsequent reports more accurately than early histories. Three analyses tested the limits of that expectation: stationary-process simulations, alternative calibration origins, and temporal generalisation of person-specific gradients. The development analyses were post hoc, and the external extension was protocol-locked but not preregistered. We therefore treat the study as a cross-dataset evaluation of a measurement choice, not as validation of a clinical tool.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study design and research questions}

This secondary study compared two representations of a person's prior reports. One remained fixed after the initial calibration period; the other moved forward with the recent history. The primary analysis compared their accuracy for the next completed report using the same number of observations. Three boundary analyses examined stationary persistence, alternative calibration origins, and the temporal stability of person-specific gradients.

Reporting was informed by STROBE and relevant prediction-model guidance \cite{ref14,ref15}. These guidelines were used to improve transparency; their citation does not imply that a nonclinical secondary analysis satisfies every item intended for clinical prediction studies.

The question developed iteratively. CES and Marian informed the initial idea, and an earlier Dejonckheere analysis tested a related updating rule before its outcome associations were inspected. The equal-weight comparison, stationary nulls, and original robustness analyses were defined later and were not preregistered. On 5 October 2026, we audited only the structure, completeness, scale ranges, and duplicate pattern of Corona Health. We then froze its outcome, eligibility, estimands, and sensitivity analyses before computing B8--L8 errors. This sequence makes Corona Health a protocol-locked external extension rather than a preregistered confirmation. The existing OSF registration concerned a different activity-and-sleep study \cite{ref27}. Supplementary Methods 1 and Tables S1--S2 in Additional file 1 document the full chronology, sample flow, and variable mapping.

Figure~\ref{fig:design} summarizes the two reference rules, the measurement timescales, and the status of the analyses.

\subsection{Data sources and participants}

\textbf{Dejonckheere/openESM 0012.} The harmonised dataset contains 100 participants from a community sample, 9,800 scheduled rows over 14 days, and seven stratified-random prompts per day \cite{ref13,ref16}. Across the retained outcomes, 8,702 reports were complete (88.8\%). Momentary happiness, relaxation, sadness, anger, and stress were scored from 0 (not at all) to 100 (very much). Every participant contributed at least nine complete reports for each retained outcome. After the first eight complete observations, each outcome had 7,902 observed targets. Sadness and stress were the frozen primary outcomes; the other three were secondary outcomes.

\textbf{College Experience Study.} CES followed undergraduate students with repeated smartphone-based assessments over four years \cite{ref11}. The supplied derived table contained 5,894 visits from 110 participants; the existing panel-construction procedure retained 5,407 visits. We analysed the two PHQ-2 items as a 0--6 score referring to the previous two weeks. The analysis included 105 participants with at least nine retained PHQ-2 assessments and 4,541 post-calibration targets. The within-person slope analysis required at least five targets and age variation, leaving 99 participants and 4,523 targets. A PHQ-2-only reconstruction, using the same interval rule for visits adjacent in the source data, produced identical target keys. Preprocessing upstream of the supplied table could not be audited. Public demographic fields were available for 104 of the 105 participants (67.6\% female, 30.5\% male, 1.0\% coded ``both'', and 1.0\% missing; 61.9\% were coded White and 22.9\% Asian). PHQ-2 is a symptom screener rather than a clinical diagnosis. The constructed panel did not permit a comparable opportunity-level nonresponse denominator, so CES completion should not be compared directly with the two scheduled EMA grids.

\textbf{Marian/openESM 0052.} The harmonised dataset contains 145 undergraduate students observed over 21 days on a 9,135-row, 63-opportunity grid \cite{ref12,ref17,ref22}. We retained 8,231 complete responses (status 1; 90.1\%) to the anhedonia and depressed-mood items. Each item used four ordered categories and referred to the preceding four hours. These adapted items are not interchangeable with the standard PHQ-2 or with Dejonckheere's ``right now'' ratings. Every participant contributed at least nine complete reports; after the first eight, each outcome had 7,071 observed targets. Depressed mood was the primary outcome and anhedonia the secondary outcome.

\textbf{Corona Health external extension.} The open Corona Health dataset contains longitudinal smartphone-app assessments from adults aged 18 years or older in Germany \cite{ref24,ref28}. The EMA file contained 11,541 rows from 1,488 anonymised user identifiers. The source documentation notes that reinstalling the app or changing phones could generate a new identifier; identifier counts therefore need not equal counts of distinct natural persons \cite{ref28}. We summed the nine PHQ-9 items when all were present and within 0--3; the app wording referred to the previous week rather than the standard two weeks. Forty-seven duplicate identifier--timestamp groups had identical item values and were collapsed. The frozen primary analysis retained 324 identifiers with at least nine complete PHQ-9 assessments and 5,618 post-calibration targets. PHQ-9 age-gradient analyses retained 232 identifiers and 5,426 targets. GAD-7 was fixed as a secondary construct-generalisation outcome before comparison. Assessments were irregular and self-selected; the median interval between consecutive complete PHQ-9 assessments was approximately seven days. Throughout the analysis, an anonymised identifier was treated as the clustering and resampling unit.

Analyses were restricted to participants who provided at least eight complete reports and one subsequent complete target. The results describe people who remained observable beyond calibration and do not estimate performance after early disengagement. In CES, the 14--30-day rule was applied to visits that were adjacent in the source data during panel construction. Removing intermediate visits created longer intervals in the retained panel: 256 post-calibration gaps exceeded 30 days, with a maximum of 506 days. Every target occurred at least 14 days after the preceding retained assessment, so its PHQ-2 recall period did not overlap that nearest history assessment. In Corona Health, weekly symptom recall could overlap across closely spaced assessments. The external analysis tests whether the prediction pattern recurs under this schedule, not whether adjacent reporting windows are independent.

\subsection{Frozen and recent personal baselines}

For participant $i$, let $y_{ij}$ be the $j$th complete report in chronological order. The frozen early baseline was

\begin{equation}
B8_i = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{8} y_{ij}.
\label{eq:b8}
\end{equation}

For a target at complete-report index $t>8$, the recent rolling baseline was

\begin{equation}
L8_{it} = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=t-8}^{t-1} y_{ij}.
\label{eq:l8}
\end{equation}

Both predictors used eight reports that preceded the target. This shared budget was retained from the established analysis pipeline because it left evaluation data in all four cohorts; it was not prospectively optimised. The early baseline ($B8_i$) represented the person near study entry and remained fixed. The recent baseline ($L8_{it}$) represented the immediately preceding part of the series and changed after each completed report. Holding the report count and estimator constant isolated the temporal location of the information. Analyses with 4, 6, and 12 reports assessed sensitivity to this choice.

For each target, update benefit was

\begin{equation}
U_{it}=|y_{it}-B8_i|-|y_{it}-L8_{it}|,
\label{eq:update}
\end{equation}

so that positive values indicate lower absolute error for the recent baseline.

\subsection{Baseline age and within-person slopes}

Baseline age measured how far each target lay from the participant's eighth complete report. CES and Corona Health used elapsed days. Dejonckheere and Marian lacked comparable raw notification timestamps in the harmonised releases, so age was the difference in scheduled opportunity count. Within each dataset--outcome pair, age was transformed with $\log(1+\mathrm{age})$ and divided by its target-level standard deviation. This scaling supports within-outcome interpretation; raw slope magnitudes were not pooled across instruments.

For every participant with at least five eligible targets and variation in age, we fitted an ordinary least-squares slope of $U_{it}$ on that participant's mean-centred standardised age. The group summary was the arithmetic mean of these participant-specific slopes, giving equal weight to participants. Five targets permit slope estimation but provide limited within-person precision. We treated the slopes as secondary descriptions of the group pattern and tested their temporal stability in non-overlapping blocks.

Participant-balanced MAE was the arithmetic mean of participant-specific MAEs. Participant-balanced RMSE was the square root of the arithmetic mean of participants' mean squared errors; it was not the arithmetic mean of participant-specific RMSEs. Relative error change was $(\mathrm{L8}-\mathrm{B8})/\mathrm{B8}\times100\%$; negative values favour L8.

\subsection{Competing explanations and alternative reference rules}

A recent history can outperform an early history under ordinary temporal persistence. We fitted a stationary Gaussian AR(1) process to each participant and outcome, preserving the observed sequence length and estimated first-order dependence. Five thousand simulated sequences yielded a reference distribution for the B8--L8 advantage under the fitted stationary process. Gaussian simulations represent bounded scores imperfectly, so we also resampled contiguous portions of each observed series. Circular stationary bootstraps with mean block lengths of 8, 16, and 32 reports retained increasing amounts of empirical local dependence. The two null models probe different assumptions and need not agree.

We next examined whether the result was specific to the arithmetic mean. Alternative rules included the last observation, cumulative mean, exponentially weighted mean (EWM8), recent median, recent mode for bounded outcomes, and an online AR(1) predictor. Every implementable rule used only reports available before the target. Supplementary Methods 6 gives the formulas and a retrospective oracle comparator that cannot be deployed prospectively.

For PHQ-2 and the four-category Marian items, predictions were also rounded to admissible scores. Exact accuracy, accuracy within one point, and ordinal absolute error were calculated. In Corona Health, rounded predictions were evaluated by exact accuracy, accuracy within two PHQ-9 or GAD-7 points, and ordinal absolute error; Median8 was retained as a robust comparator. We additionally divided each participant's MAE by that participant's observed within-person standard deviation to aid comparison across scales. Participants with no within-person variation were excluded only from this standardised analysis.

\subsection{Calibration-origin and observation-process checks}

If the apparent ageing of a baseline reflects an unusual study-entry period, it should weaken when the calibration point is moved. We repeated the analysis from early, middle, and late pseudo-origins within each participant's observed series. Each origin used eight calibration reports, an eight-report washout, and at least five later targets. The origins shared observations and were chosen from the completed series, so this analysis assessed sensitivity to study start rather than prospective performance.

Further checks varied the history length ($K=4,6,8,$ or 12), excluded the first EMA day, adjusted retrospectively for prompt position, and reweighted observed responses. Response models used participant-level folds with no overlap between training and test participants. They included only information available by each scheduled opportunity. Estimated response probabilities were clipped to 0.10--0.99; reciprocal inverse-probability weights were then calculated and normalised within participant. These analyses assessed whether the main direction could be attributed to one window, entry-day adaptation, within-day timing, or modelled observation patterns. Full specifications are reported in Supplementary Methods 7.

\subsection{Temporal generalisability of individual differences}

The group-average benefit of updating need not describe every person. We divided each participant's post-calibration targets into non-overlapping early and late blocks. Each block contained at least five targets. Participant-specific age gradients and mean update benefits were estimated separately in the two blocks, then correlated across participants. Bootstrap intervals used 2,000 participant resamples. We interpreted these correlations as temporal repeatability of estimated task summaries, not as formal reliability coefficients or latent traits.

\subsection{Uncertainty, multiplicity, and software}

Unless otherwise stated, 95\% intervals came from 2,000 percentile bootstrap samples of participants. The participant was the resampling and weighting unit. These intervals describe uncertainty within each selected dataset, not sampling variation across datasets. Only the four development-outcome stationary challenges---sadness, stress, PHQ-2, and depressed mood---formed the primary multiplicity family; they used a Bonferroni threshold of 0.0125. The externally locked PHQ-9 analysis was evaluated separately because it was specified later under a distinct protocol. All other outcomes, comparator models, and sensitivity analyses were descriptive. Sample size was determined by the available secondary data and the frozen eligibility rules.

Analyses used Python 3.12 with random seed 20260918. Automated tests checked temporal ordering, exclusion of current and future outcomes, equal-history construction, participant-level resampling, simulation recovery, and reconciliation of reported outputs. Package versions, full formulas, cohort flow, and detailed sensitivity specifications are reported in the Supplement.

\section{Results}

The results follow the evidential questions set out above: whether recent histories improved next-report prediction, whether the difference survived stationary-process challenges, whether it depended on a particular updating rule or study origin, and whether participant-specific gradients persisted across time. Because the development analyses were post hoc, these questions organise the evidence but are not presented as preregistered hypotheses.

\subsection{Recent histories provided a better personal reference}

Across the three development samples, the same pattern emerged: a person's recent reports predicted the next report better than the person's first reports (Table~\ref{tab:main}). L8 reduced participant-balanced MAE for all eight outcomes. Relative reductions ranged from 9.97\% for sadness to 29.80\% for anhedonia, and every participant-bootstrap interval excluded zero. The differences were also present after error was scaled by each participant's within-person standard deviation (Supplementary Table S17). Outcomes from the same sample were correlated, so these results represent three development datasets rather than eight independent replications.

The Corona Health extension reproduced the direction. For PHQ-9, participant-balanced MAE fell from 2.20 to 1.83 points among 324 participants and 5,618 targets, a reduction of 16.60\% (95\% CI 13.18\% to 20.11\%). L8 had lower participant-specific MAE for 58.0\% of participants. The secondary GAD-7 result was similar (16.82\% reduction, 95\% CI 13.54\% to 20.35\%). Both outcomes came from the same cohort and do not constitute independent replications.

\subsection{The benefit extended beyond the rolling mean}

The advantage of recent information was not specific to a rolling arithmetic mean (Table~\ref{tab:comparators}). EWM8 improved on L8 for every outcome. Recent medians performed especially well for sadness and stress, while a recent median or mode was best for the floor-concentrated PHQ-2 and Marian outcomes. The last observation was also competitive in CES and Marian. The online AR(1) predictor did not consistently outperform these simpler summaries. Recent information was useful across several reference rules, although the best summary depended on the score distribution.

The same direction appeared when predictions were rounded to admissible scores. Exact accuracy increased by 9.49 percentage points for PHQ-2, 15.66 for depressed mood, and 14.83 for anhedonia. Ordinal MAE decreased by 0.25, 0.18, and 0.17 points, respectively (Supplementary Table S10). These outcomes were strongly concentrated at the scale floor (Supplementary Table S11), which helps explain why medians and modes were competitive.

\subsection{Early references became less useful as the series progressed}

The advantage of updating was larger later in the observed series. Mean within-person associations between baseline age and update benefit were positive for all eight development outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:slope}). For the four primary development outcomes, mean gradients were 0.533 for sadness, 1.568 for stress, 0.110 for PHQ-2, and 0.059 for depressed mood. The protocol-locked PHQ-9 extension yielded a mean gradient of 0.291 (95\% CI 0.168 to 0.411). Every displayed participant-bootstrap interval excluded zero. Because the outcomes used different scales and age units before standardisation, the raw gradients should not be compared directly.

Descriptive time strata made this pattern easier to see. In CES, the L8 advantage increased from 10.7\% within 90 days of calibration to 39.9\% beyond 730 days. For Marian depressed mood, it increased from 19.2\% in the earliest stratum to 49.8\% in the latest. Sadness and stress showed smaller early advantages and larger late advantages in Dejonckheere. The continuous within-person gradients, rather than these descriptive strata, were used for statistical inference.

\subsection{Stationary persistence explained part, but not all, of the advantage}

All four primary development outcomes showed positive lag-1 autocorrelation, and stationary simulations also favoured recent information. The empirical advantages were larger than the fitted Gaussian AR(1) expectations. Observed B8-minus-L8 differences were 1.174 for sadness, 3.855 for stress, 0.228 for PHQ-2, and 0.147 for depressed mood. The corresponding null means were 0.322, 0.649, 0.044, and 0.020. Each observed value exceeded all 5,000 simulated values ($p_{\mathrm{MC}}=1/5001=0.0002$), below the Bonferroni threshold of 0.0125. The observed age gradients also exceeded their simulated distributions.

Corona Health PHQ-9 showed the same ordering (Fig.~\ref{fig:external}b). After Monte Carlo correction, the observed PHQ-9 MAE advantage (0.365) exceeded all 5,000 fitted stationary Gaussian AR(1) draws (null mean 0.175, central 95\% interval 0.119 to 0.236; $p_{\mathrm{MC}}=0.0002$). The null mean was 47.9\% of the observed difference. This percentage is a descriptive comparison, not a decomposition of the observed effect: it shows that ordinary persistence generated an advantage of substantial size, while the fitted model did not reproduce its full magnitude. The comparison rejects the fitted null, not all stationary processes.

The empirical block analysis produced a more qualified result. For sadness, one-sided Monte Carlo values increased from 0.032 to 0.064 and 0.131 as mean block length increased from 8 to 16 and 32 reports. The other three primary mean benefits and all four age gradients remained beyond the simulated values at every block length ($p_{\mathrm{MC}}=0.001$). Thus, the fitted Gaussian and empirical block reference processes did not reproduce most observed differences, but the evidence was weaker for sadness. These diagnostics do not rule out other stationary processes.

\subsection{Updating remained useful after re-anchoring, but continuous ageing did not}

Recent histories remained advantageous across 4-, 6-, 8-, and 12-report windows (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}a). Relative MAE reductions ranged from 9.23\% to 11.21\% for sadness, 15.71\% to 19.38\% for stress, 22.82\% to 29.00\% for PHQ-2, and 23.98\% to 37.89\% for depressed mood. The secondary outcomes were directionally similar (Supplementary Table S13).

EMA timing and observation checks also preserved the direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}c). After day 1 was removed and calibration was repeated, MAE reductions were 10.14\% for sadness, 18.10\% for stress, 26.47\% for depressed mood, and 25.63\% for anhedonia. Prompt-position adjustment and inverse-observation weighting produced similar estimates (Supplementary Tables S15 and S18). Every CES target was at least 14 days after the preceding retained PHQ-2 assessment.

Moving the calibration point altered the age pattern more than the prediction pattern (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}b). At late origins, recent history still reduced MAE for stress (11.59\%), PHQ-2 (13.69\%), and depressed mood (21.42\%). The sadness estimate was imprecise. Most late-origin age-gradient intervals included zero, and the anhedonia gradient became negative. Updating remained useful in most outcomes, but evidence for continuous loss of value depended partly on the original study start.

\subsection{Group-level change did not imply a stable personal decay rate}

Positive mean gradients did not imply that every participant followed the same pattern. Between 59.0\% and 71.0\% of participants had positive gradients, depending on outcome. Participant-specific gradient ranks also failed to persist across non-overlapping time blocks. Early--late correlations were $-0.100$ for sadness, $-0.084$ for stress, $-0.281$ for PHQ-2, and 0.083 for depressed mood. Their bootstrap intervals did not support temporal generalisation. Although these estimates were noisy, they provided no evidence for a stable personal rate of baseline ageing.

Mean update benefits were more repeatable. Early--late correlations ranged from 0.496 to 0.748 in raw units and from 0.645 to 0.793 after within-person standardisation. Both blocks used the same finite B8 reference, so these correlations partly reflect a shared benchmark and should not be interpreted as an independent psychological trait.

Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity} contrasts the clear group-average pattern with the weak temporal stability of person-specific gradients.

Two exploratory analyses clarified what the group result could not establish. First, a nested participant-held-out ridge model used only summaries of the first eight reports to predict later update benefit. It passed the prespecified exploratory gate for sadness and depressed mood, but failed for stress and PHQ-2 (Supplementary Table S5). Early-report features therefore did not identify future beneficiaries consistently across outcomes. Second, a semi-synthetic sustained-worsening stress test exposed a safety trade-off. The recent-eight reference retained only 0.164 of the imposed plateau contrast, compared with 1.000 for the fixed early reference, even while reducing ordinary next-report error (Supplementary Tables S6--S7). These analyses were post hoc and were not used to support the main findings; they show why predictive accuracy and change detection must be evaluated as separate tasks.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What changes when a personal baseline is updated?}

Across three development datasets and the Corona Health extension, recent reports provided a more accurate reference for the next report than the same number of reports collected near study entry. The advantage ranged from roughly 10\% to 30\% across momentary affect, stress, PHQ-2, anhedonia, depressed mood, PHQ-9, and GAD-7. It also remained under alternative window lengths, calibration origins, long-gap rules, and summary statistics. The key implication is that a personal baseline is defined by both whose data it contains and when those data were collected.

This distinction matters because a fixed baseline can look individualized while behaving like a rigid norm. It preserves between-person differences, but it assumes that the person's early reporting pattern remains the appropriate reference. The present results instead support a temporally local view of psychological measurement. Earlier and current observations belong to the same individual, yet they need not carry equal information about the individual's next report. This interpretation is compatible with work showing that within-person dynamics depend on measurement timescale and cannot be inferred from between-person differences alone \cite{ref4,ref5,ref6}.

These results also clarify how updating should be implemented in practice. More complex models were not consistently better. Recent medians or modes often outperformed recent means when scores were bounded and concentrated at the floor. Researchers must choose both when to update and how to summarise recent experience on the scale being measured. This pattern supports treating behavioural baselines as task- and context-specific forecasting rules instead of fixed personal properties \cite{ref20}.

\subsection{Why did the early reference lose predictive value?}

Temporal proximity explained part of the difference. Stationary AR(1) simulations favoured recent observations even though their generating processes did not change over time. For PHQ-9, the fitted null mean was roughly half the observed difference. The gap between those quantities shows what that particular stationary model failed to reproduce; it is not evidence that the remainder arose from a single non-stationary mechanism. The observed advantages also exceeded empirical block-bootstrap expectations for stress, PHQ-2, and depressed mood. Evidence was weaker for sadness because its result depended on the amount of dependence retained by the bootstrap. A stationary first-order account was insufficient for most outcomes, although the analyses do not identify a common psychological mechanism.

Several processes could make early reports less representative. Over years, students encounter new academic, social, and health contexts. Over days, experience-sampling participants may adapt to the study, change their use of the response scale, or move through short affective episodes. Regression after an unusual entry period, nonlinear dependence, seasonality, and selective responding could produce similar patterns. Recent information usually retained an advantage after re-anchoring, whereas the steady age gradient weakened. The data support updating as a predictive strategy, but not a universal process of progressive baseline decay.

\subsection{The group pattern was not an individual trait}

The lack of stable person-specific gradients provides a second important boundary. Some participants showed increasingly large benefits from updating, whereas others did not. A participant's estimated gradient in the first half of the series did not predict the gradient in the second half. Group-level ageing of a reference should not be converted into a personal ``decay rate,'' because the present estimates did not behave as stable individual characteristics.

This result changes how personalisation should be evaluated. Estimating a separate parameter for every person is useful only if that parameter remains informative later. In these data, a shared updating rule was more defensible than assigning stable rates of baseline ageing to individuals. Future adaptive-monitoring studies should evaluate person-specific rules in later, non-overlapping periods and in new participants.

The sustained-change experiment adds a complementary warning. An adaptive reference reduces error from an outdated benchmark, but it can also absorb a persistent shift that an early reference would continue to display. Next-report prediction and detection of departure from an earlier state are distinct measurement tasks. An alerting system may therefore need a recent forecasting reference alongside a slower or fixed change-detection reference. The semi-synthetic test illustrates this design tension and does not establish a clinical alarm threshold.

\subsection{Strengths, limitations, and next steps}

The controlled comparison between two equally simple references is the study's main strength. Both used eight prior reports and the same estimator, while participants determined the weight of the analysis. The development datasets covered momentary ratings collected several times per day and symptoms measured over several years. The independently sourced Corona Health cohort extended the comparison to complete PHQ-9 and GAD-7 item sets. Stationary null models, alternative origins, long-gap reconstruction, and robust summaries tested whether the initial pattern survived plausible analytic challenges.

Several limitations qualify the interpretation. The core equal-weight analysis and its original robustness checks were post hoc. The Corona Health protocol was locked before its B8--L8 comparison, but only after the question and analytic family had been developed. It offers stronger protection than an unrestricted post hoc analysis, yet less than prospective preregistration. Differences in population, scale, recall period, and assessment schedule limit agreement to a temporal measurement pattern rather than a common disorder process. Corona Health used a one-week instruction, and neighbouring recall windows could overlap. Rounded predictions and robust location summaries supported the arithmetic-error analyses of bounded outcomes, but they do not replace a full ordinal measurement model. Eligibility required continued participation, and weighting cannot recover unreported psychological states. CES lacked a comparable scheduled-opportunity denominator, and its upstream preprocessing could not be fully audited.

The null models provide useful challenges but not a definitive account of temporal dependence. The Gaussian AR(1) model did not reproduce several bounded marginal features. For example, the observed versus simulated floor fractions were 0.703 versus 0.289 for Marian depressed mood, and 6.3\% of simulated Corona PHQ-9 values fell outside the admissible 0--27 range because that generator was not clipped (Supplementary Table S23). The block bootstrap preserved the observed score grid but depended on uncalibrated block lengths. Pseudo-origins shared observations and were chosen retrospectively. These analyses narrow the plausible interpretation, but a prospective design is needed to separate baseline age from study position.

Future studies should specify reference rules before inspecting outcomes and use staggered or randomised calibration origins where possible. They should distinguish next-report prediction from detection of sustained, clinically meaningful deterioration. The 0.365-point PHQ-9 result is a statistically reliable difference in prediction error, not a change in a participant's symptom score, and should not be interpreted against symptom-change thresholds. Its clinical importance remains unknown because this study did not test whether the reduction improves screening, triage, feedback, or monitoring decisions. Clinical use would require prespecified events, action thresholds, and direct evidence of such decision benefit.

\section{Conclusions}

Across three development datasets and an externally locked cohort, recent histories provided a more accurate next-report reference than equally sized histories collected near study entry. The advantage appeared across timescales and updating rules, although its size depended on the outcome and stationary comparator. Re-anchoring weakened evidence for continuous baseline ageing, and person-specific gradients did not generalise across time. Personal baselines are therefore time-located measurement choices, not timeless summaries of an individual. Prospective evidence is required before this principle is used for clinical monitoring.
