\title[Early versus recent personal histories]{Early versus recent personal histories in repeated psychological self-reports: a cross-timescale secondary analysis}

\author[1]{\fnm{Runqing} \sur{Liao}}\email{202411055222@stu.hstc.edu.cn}
\author*[1]{\fnm{Zhining} \sur{Wang}}\email{mathwangzhining@163.com}
\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{School of Mathematics and Statistics}, \orgname{Hanshan Normal University}, \orgaddress{\city{Chaozhou}, \postcode{521041}, \country{China}}}

\abstract{\textbf{Background:} Repeated psychological assessments are often interpreted relative to a person's earlier reports. A recent history can predict the next report better simply because psychological responses are autocorrelated, even when the data-generating process is stationary. We therefore tested whether an early eight-report reference was less predictive than an equally sized recent history, and whether the observed difference exceeded that expected under stationary within-person persistence.

\textbf{Methods:} Secondary analyses used three selected longitudinal samples: Dejonckheere/openESM ($n=100$), the College Experience Study (CES; $n=105$), and Marian/openESM ($n=145$). For each later report, we compared the mean of the first eight complete reports (B8) with the eight most recent complete reports (L8). Outcomes comprised five momentary affect or stress ratings, Patient Health Questionnaire-2 scores, and two momentary depression-related items. Participant-bootstrap intervals quantified uncertainty. A participant-matched stationary autoregressive AR(1) parametric bootstrap tested whether short-range persistence alone could reproduce the observed advantages. Post hoc analyses compared leakage-safe recent-history rules and examined window length, alternative calibration origins, observation weighting, first-day exclusion, and prompt timing. Only the Dejonckheere test was outcome-association-blind.

\textbf{Results:} Across three samples and eight measured outcomes, L8 reduced participant-balanced mean absolute error relative to B8 by 9.97\%--29.80\%. In the four primary outcomes, absolute B8-minus-L8 advantages were 1.17 sadness points, 3.85 stress points, 0.23 PHQ-2 points, and 0.15 depressed-mood points. Each exceeded its stationary AR(1) null distribution (5,000 draws; one-sided Monte Carlo $p=0.0002$), although the null itself favoured recent observations. First-day exclusion and prompt-adjusted EMA analyses preserved the direction. Median or modal recent-history rules were more accurate than L8 for the floor-concentrated PHQ-2 and four-category outcomes, showing that the arithmetic rolling mean was not uniquely optimal. After resetting the calibration origin, recent-history advantages remained for stress, PHQ-2, and depressed mood but were inconclusive for sadness; age gradients attenuated and did not generalize reliably within persons.

\textbf{Conclusions:} In these selected samples, recent histories carried predictive information beyond that expected from a fitted stationary AR(1) process, but this does not establish a universal decay of personal baselines. Predictor choice also depended on scale properties and the intended use of the reference. Early-history, recent-history, and robust location summaries should be compared explicitly in prospective studies before clinical interpretation.}

\section{Background}

When psychological states are measured repeatedly, a person's earlier answers provide a natural point of reference. A sadness score of 40, for example, may carry a different meaning for someone who usually reports very little sadness than for someone whose reports are commonly higher. Personal baselines are therefore attractive in longitudinal research: they can reduce stable differences between people and make current observations interpretable relative to the person's own history. This logic underlies many idiographic, ecological momentary assessment (EMA), and personalised prediction approaches \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3}.

The usefulness of a personal reference nevertheless depends on an assumption that is often left implicit: earlier observations must remain representative of the person at the time of prediction. Psychological states vary within individuals, and their dynamics depend on the time scale of measurement \cite{ref4,ref5,ref6}. A reference estimated near the beginning of a study may reflect a transient period, an earlier academic or social context, or a different phase of symptoms. We therefore distinguish an \emph{early-history reference}, fixed at the first reports, from a \emph{recent-history reference}, recalculated from the reports immediately preceding a target. Their difference in absolute prediction error is termed \emph{update benefit}. These are operational labels rather than claims that time causes psychological change or that a latent personal baseline literally decays.

Existing longitudinal models provide several ways to use personal history. A fixed person mean emphasizes stable differences, the most recent observation emphasizes short-term continuity, and time-series or multilevel models combine longer and shorter histories \cite{ref2,ref5,ref7}. Studies using smartphones and wearables have likewise reported heterogeneous benefits from person-specific modelling \cite{ref8,ref9,ref10}. Temporal windows can change which psychological associations are visible \cite{ref18}, and leakage-prone validation can make complex mobile-health models look better than simple heuristics \cite{ref19}. Recent work has therefore framed individual baselines as forecasting rules whose validity depends on the task, person, context, and updating scheme \cite{ref20}. A recent-history predictor, however, has a built-in advantage under ordinary stationary autocorrelation: nearer observations correlate more strongly with the next report even if no long-term change has occurred. A persuasive analysis must consequently hold information quantity constant and ask whether the empirical advantage is larger than this stationary expectation.

A stricter comparison holds the information budget constant. Both predictors use exactly eight earlier reports and the same arithmetic-mean estimator: one remains fixed at the first eight reports, whereas the other uses the eight reports immediately preceding each target. This isolates temporal location from information quantity, but it does not by itself isolate nonstationarity from autocorrelation. We therefore paired the empirical comparison with participant-matched stationary AR(1) simulations. Evidence for time-varying relevance requires the observed recent-history advantage to exceed the advantage generated by fitted stationary persistence, while alternative calibration origins test whether any apparent age gradient is specific to the original study start.

The present study examined these questions in three datasets that differ in measurement cadence and outcome. CES supplied repeated PHQ-2 assessments over months and years \cite{ref11}; Marian supplied momentary anhedonia and depressed-mood items over 21 days in undergraduate students \cite{ref12}; and Dejonckheere supplied momentary sadness, stress, happiness, relaxation, and anger ratings seven times per day for 14 days in a community sample \cite{ref13}. These samples were selected because the available files contained repeated self-report outcomes, participant identifiers, temporal ordering, and enough within-person observations for equal-history prediction. Other available digital-phenotyping datasets were not included because their focal outcomes, sampling grids, or usable repeated-report structures would have introduced a different question. Dejonckheere provided the only outcome-association-blind, protocol-frozen test; CES and Marian supplied development and post hoc cross-timescale extensions. This staged design is not an independently preregistered replication. We asked whether recent history outperformed early history, whether the advantage exceeded stationary AR(1) persistence, whether it depended on the original calibration origin, and whether person-specific differences generalized over time.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study design and evidential chronology}

This was a secondary analysis of de-identified longitudinal self-report datasets. The scientific question was developed iteratively. CES and Marian first showed that continuously updated self-report history outperformed a frozen early history, but that comparison allowed the updated predictor to use more reports. Before testing the central association in Dejonckheere, we froze the outcomes, eight-report calibration budget, participant-level validation, baseline-age definition, missingness sensitivity, bootstrap procedure, and success criterion. After inspecting that result, we specified the equal-information extensions in CES and Marian. The stationary AR(1) null, expanded comparators, first-day and prompt-timing checks, pseudo-origins, and temporal-generalizability analyses were subsequently defined in dated post hoc protocols. None was covered prospectively by the earlier embargoed OSF registration (\url{https://osf.io/n2j3m/}), which concerned a separate CES--PSYCHE-D activity and sleep question. Versioned protocols improve traceability but do not make these analyses preregistered or confirmatory.

Reporting was informed by STROBE and relevant prediction-model guidance \cite{ref14,ref15}. These guidelines were used to improve transparency; their citation does not imply that a nonclinical secondary analysis satisfies every item intended for clinical prediction studies.

Figure~\ref{fig:design} summarizes the equal-information comparison, the three measurement timescales, and the evidential chronology. Detailed sample flow, variable mapping, and analysis chronology are provided in Supplementary Tables S1--S2 and Supplementary Methods 1.

\subsection{Data sources and participants}

\textbf{Dejonckheere/openESM 0012.} The harmonized dataset contains 100 participants from a community sample, 9,800 scheduled rows over 14 days, and seven stratified-random prompts per day \cite{ref13,ref16}. Across the retained outcomes, 8,702 reports were complete (88.8\%). Momentary happiness, relaxation, sadness, anger, and stress were scored from 0 (not at all) to 100 (very much). Every participant contributed at least nine complete reports for each retained outcome. After the first eight complete observations, each outcome had 7,902 observed targets. Sadness and stress were the protocol-frozen primary outcomes; the other three were secondary outcomes.

\textbf{College Experience Study.} CES followed undergraduate students with repeated smartphone-based assessments over four years \cite{ref11}. The constructed PHQ-2 panel contained 5,407 complete assessments from 110 participants. We analysed the two PHQ-2 items as a 0--6 score referring to the previous two weeks. The equal-information analysis included 105 participants with at least nine complete PHQ-2 assessments and 4,541 post-calibration targets. The within-person slope analysis required at least five targets and age variation, leaving 99 participants and 4,523 targets. Public demographic fields were available for 104 of the 105 analytic participants (67.6\% female, 30.5\% male, 1.0\% coded ``both'', and 1.0\% missing; 61.9\% were coded White and 22.9\% Asian). PHQ-2 is a symptom screener rather than a clinical diagnosis. The constructed panel did not permit a fully comparable opportunity-level nonresponse denominator, so CES completion should not be compared directly with the two scheduled EMA grids.

\textbf{Marian/openESM 0052.} The harmonized dataset contains 145 undergraduate students observed over 21 days on a 9,135-row, 63-opportunity grid \cite{ref12,ref17}. We retained 8,231 complete responses (status 1; 90.1\%) to the anhedonia and depressed-mood items, each scored on four ordered categories. These items were adapted for momentary assessment and are not interchangeable with the standard PHQ-2. Every participant contributed at least nine complete reports; after the first eight, each outcome had 7,071 observed targets. Depressed mood was the primary replication outcome and anhedonia was secondary.

The analytic samples condition on providing at least eight complete reports and at least one subsequent complete target. Consequently, the analyses describe participants who remained observable beyond calibration; they do not estimate performance for people who disengaged earlier. In CES, all post-calibration targets occurred at least 14 days after the immediately preceding complete assessment, so the target PHQ-2 recall period did not overlap the nearest history assessment.

\subsection{Frozen and recent personal baselines}

For participant $i$, let $y_{ij}$ be the $j$th complete report in chronological order. The frozen early baseline was

\begin{equation}
B8_i = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{8} y_{ij}.
\label{eq:b8}
\end{equation}

For a target at complete-report index $t>8$, the recent rolling baseline was

\begin{equation}
L8_{it} = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=t-8}^{t-1} y_{ij}.
\label{eq:l8}
\end{equation}

Both predictors therefore contained exactly eight strictly prior values and used the same arithmetic-mean estimator. The current target and all future observations were unavailable when constructing either baseline. $B8_i$ remained unchanged for all later targets, whereas $L8_{it}$ moved forward by one complete observation. Earlier training-fold-weighted variants that combined the window mean with the most recent report were retained as supporting analyses; the equal-weight comparison in Eqs.~\ref{eq:b8}--\ref{eq:l8} was chosen for the clearest common interpretation.

For each target, update benefit was

\begin{equation}
U_{it}=|y_{it}-B8_i|-|y_{it}-L8_{it}|,
\label{eq:update}
\end{equation}

so that positive values indicate lower absolute error for the recent baseline.

\subsection{Baseline age and within-person slopes}

Baseline age measured how far each target lay from the participant's eighth complete report. CES used elapsed days. Dejonckheere and Marian lacked comparable raw notification timestamps in the harmonized releases, so age was the difference in scheduled opportunity counter. Within each dataset--outcome pair, age was transformed with $\log(1+\mathrm{age})$ and divided by its target-level standard deviation. This scaling supports within-outcome interpretation; raw slope magnitudes were not pooled across instruments.

For every participant with at least five eligible targets and variation in age, we fitted an ordinary least-squares slope of $U_{it}$ on that participant's mean-centred standardized age. The group summary was the arithmetic mean of these participant-specific slopes, so participants rather than repeated observations received equal weight. Five targets permit slope estimation but provide limited within-person precision; the slopes were therefore treated as secondary descriptions of the group pattern, not as reliable personal traits. We also recorded the proportion of participants with positive slopes and tested temporal stability in non-overlapping blocks.

Participant-balanced mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) were calculated by first computing each error metric within participant and then averaging across participants. Relative error change was $(\mathrm{L8}-\mathrm{B8})/\mathrm{B8}\times100\%$; negative values favour L8.

\subsection{Post hoc comparator and bounded-score analyses}

We added strictly prior, leakage-safe comparators to determine whether the result was specific to the arithmetic mean. The last-observation predictor used $y_{i,t-1}$, and the cumulative predictor averaged all complete reports before the target. EWM8 used the fixed smoothing constant $\alpha=2/(8+1)$, was initialized at B8, and was updated only after the current target had been scored. Median8 used the median of the preceding eight reports; for PHQ-2 and the four-category Marian outcomes, Mode8 used the modal value with deterministic lower-value tie-breaking. An online AR(1) predictor re-estimated an intercept and lag coefficient from strictly prior reports at each target, with a minimum of eight prior observations and coefficient clipping to the stationary range. An oracle participant mean, computed from the complete series, was included only as a non-deployable diagnostic ceiling and was never interpreted as a valid prospective predictor. No parameter was tuned to outcome performance. These analyses were post hoc.

For PHQ-2 and the two four-category Marian items, we additionally rounded B8 and L8 predictions to the nearest admissible category or total score and compared exact accuracy, accuracy within one score point, and ordinal absolute error. These category-level summaries test whether the B8--L8 direction survives bounded scoring; they are not latent-variable or measurement-invariance analyses. To improve cross-scale interpretation, MAE was also divided by each participant's observed within-person standard deviation before averaging; participants with zero within-person variance were excluded only from this standardized estimand. We tabulated score means, standard deviations, floor and ceiling frequencies, source rows, complete records, and exclusions. All added intervals used 2,000 participant bootstrap resamples with the same seed and equal-participant estimand as the primary analysis.

\subsection{Stationary-process null and sensitivity analyses}

The principal post hoc null was a participant-matched stationary Gaussian AR(1) parametric bootstrap. For each participant and outcome, we estimated the mean, variance, and lag-1 coefficient from the complete observed sequence. Lag coefficients were partially pooled towards the outcome-level estimate and clipped to $[-0.95,0.95]$ to avoid unstable boundary simulations. We generated 5,000 stationary sequences with the observed participant-specific length and missing-position pattern, then reconstructed B8, L8, participant-balanced MAE advantage, and the mean within-person age gradient. This null deliberately permits a recent-history advantage caused by stationary persistence. One-sided Monte Carlo values used $(1+k)/5001$. We report observed effects, null means, central 95\% null intervals, and standardized distances from the null. The model is a falsification device rather than a claim that psychological responses are Gaussian or exactly AR(1).

As descriptive diagnostics, we estimated each outcome's participant-mean lag-1 autocorrelation, the between-person share of total variance, and participant-specific linear trend. Earlier complete time permutations and circular shifts are retained in the Supplement because permutation destroys persistence and circular shifting creates an artificial wrap boundary; neither is the primary null after review.

Dejonckheere additionally used an inverse-probability-of-observation sensitivity analysis. Completion was modelled among training participants from strictly prior completion history, study day, and prompt number; predicted probabilities were truncated to 0.10--0.99. This analysis depends on a missing-at-random assumption conditional on observed history and cannot recover unreported psychological states.

\subsection{Post hoc origin, window, and observation sensitivities}

Additional analyses were specified in dated protocols before their execution and are reported as post hoc sensitivities. In CES, an audit established that every scored target was at least 14 days after the preceding complete PHQ-2 assessment; because no shorter-gap comparison existed, this was treated as a design property rather than a sensitivity contrast. Marian equal-weight targets were reweighted by participant-disjoint response probabilities estimated from strictly prior completion rate, missing streak, study day, and prompt number. Probabilities were truncated to 0.10--0.99 and weights were normalized within participant. Early and recent equal-weight means were also compared for $K\in\{4,6,8,12\}$ on a common target set beginning after 12 complete reports.

The first eight complete EMA reports covered a median of two calendar days in Dejonckheere and three days in Marian. To test whether this short calibration period was dominated by entry-day adaptation, we removed all day-1 observations, re-established B8 from the next eight complete reports, and repeated the comparison. To address systematic within-day rhythms, we regressed each EMA outcome on prompt position using only strictly prior eligible data and repeated B8--L8 prediction on prompt-adjusted residuals.

Fourth, we repeated the analysis after defining early, middle, and late pseudo-origins within each participant's complete-report sequence. Each pseudo-origin used eight reports for B8, omitted the next eight reports from evaluation, and required at least five later targets. The washout removed the mechanically identical first B8 and L8 prediction. We estimated both relative MAE and participant-specific age slopes from each origin. Origin placement depended on the retrospectively observed complete-series length, and origins shared participants and observations. This diagnostic therefore tests sensitivity to the original study start; it is neither an independent replication nor prospective rolling-origin validation.

\subsection{Temporal generalizability of person-specific summaries}

To test whether age gradients were temporally generalizable, each eligible participant's post-calibration targets were divided chronologically into non-overlapping early and late halves, with at least five targets per half. Update-benefit slopes and mean update benefits were estimated separately within each block. We calculated participant-level Pearson and Spearman correlations between blocks, with 2,000 participant-bootstrap resamples for Pearson intervals. Mean benefit was examined both in raw units and after division by the participant's within-person standard deviation. These correlations describe rank stability of noisy estimates; they are not reliability coefficients from a prespecified measurement model. A frozen gate required all four primary slope correlations to be positive with lower interval bounds above zero before individual slope prediction would be considered.

For completeness, the Supplement retains a deterministic sustained-change exercise and an exploratory ridge analysis that were performed earlier in development. Because the signal-retention result is largely determined by the imposed ramp and window, and the ridge target is mathematically coupled to B8, neither is used to support the principal claims.

\subsection{Uncertainty, multiplicity, and software}

Unless otherwise stated, 95\% intervals were obtained from 2,000 percentile bootstrap samples of participants. Bootstrap intervals describe uncertainty among the observed participants while holding the selected datasets and analysis decisions fixed; they are not random-dataset generalization intervals. The eight B8--L8 contrasts are complete descriptive outcome estimates. Because only the Dejonckheere test was outcome-association-blind and the stationary AR(1) model was introduced after initial results, no analysis is relabelled as confirmatory. The four primary AR(1) comparisons are reported as post hoc falsification tests with Monte Carlo values at the resolution supported by 5,000 draws. Other analyses are interpreted from effect magnitude, interval width, and consistency. Exact participant and target counts are reported together throughout. Because these were fixed secondary datasets, no prospective power calculation determined sample size; eligibility and released-data availability determined $n$.

Analyses used Python 3.12, pandas 2.3.3, NumPy 2.3.5, scikit-learn 1.7.2, and matplotlib, with random seed 20260918. Automated tests checked temporal ordering, current/future-label exclusion, participant separation, equal-history construction, stationary-simulation recovery, common targets across window lengths, pseudo-origin washout, semi-synthetic injection ordering, participant-level resampling, and output reconciliation. Full formulas, robustness specifications, software environment, cohort flow, absolute and standardized performance, bounded-score results, and post hoc sensitivities are reported in the Supplement.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Recent eight-report baselines reduced prediction error}

Across the three samples and all eight measured outcomes, L8 had lower participant-balanced error than B8 (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Absolute B8-minus-L8 MAE differences ranged from 1.09 to 3.85 points on the 0--100 Dejonckheere scales, were 0.23 points on the 0--6 PHQ-2 scale, and were 0.15--0.17 points on the 1--4 Marian scales. Relative reductions ranged from 9.97\% for sadness to 29.80\% for anhedonia. Every participant-bootstrap interval excluded zero. Participant-SD-standardized analyses preserved the same direction (Supplementary Table S17). The eight outcomes are correlated within their source samples and therefore constitute three samples, not eight independent replications.

\subsection{Recent history reduced error but did not identify a uniquely optimal rule}

The post hoc comparisons qualified the primary finding (Table~\ref{tab:comparators}). EWM8 improved on L8 in every outcome. Median8 performed particularly well for sadness and stress, and median or mode was best among tested deployable rules for the floor-concentrated PHQ-2 and Marian outcomes. The last observation was also competitive for CES and Marian. Online AR(1) did not dominate the simpler robust summaries, and the diagnostic oracle mean was not consistently better than leakage-safe local rules (Supplementary Table S17). Thus, the B8--L8 contrast shows that temporal location matters under an equal information budget; it does not establish that a rolling arithmetic mean is optimal.

Rounding predictions to admissible scores supported the B8--L8 direction for bounded outcomes. Exact accuracy increased by 9.49 percentage points for PHQ-2, 15.66 for depressed mood, and 14.83 for anhedonia; ordinal MAE decreased by 0.25, 0.18, and 0.17 score points, respectively. Strong floor concentrations---48.8\% for PHQ-2, 71.5\% for depressed mood, and 65.5\% for anhedonia---remain an important interpretive limitation (Supplementary Table S11).

\subsection{The advantage of updating increased with baseline age}

The mean within-person association between baseline age and update benefit was positive for all eight outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:slope}). For the four primary outcomes, mean slopes were 0.533 for sadness, 1.568 for stress, 0.110 for PHQ-2, and 0.059 for momentary depressed mood; each participant-bootstrap interval excluded zero. The units are outcome-specific absolute-error points per one-standard-deviation increase in log baseline age and should not be compared as standardized effect sizes across scales.

Descriptive strata showed the same pattern. In CES, the MAE advantage of L8 over B8 increased from approximately 10.7\% within 90 days of the eighth report to 39.9\% beyond 730 days. For Marian depressed mood, it increased from 19.2\% in the earliest opportunity stratum to 49.8\% in the latest. In Dejonckheere, sadness improved by 4.5\% in the earliest stratum and 18.7\% in the latest; stress improved by 7.0\% and 27.4\%, respectively. These strata were descriptive; the continuous within-person slopes were the inferential quantities.

\subsection{Observed advantages exceeded a stationary AR(1) expectation}

Autocorrelation was present but modest: participant-mean lag-1 correlations were 0.179 for sadness, 0.226 for stress, 0.261 for PHQ-2, and 0.246 for depressed mood. The descriptive between-person shares of total variance were 0.229, 0.263, 0.473, and 0.339, respectively (Supplementary Table S16 and archived diagnostic output). Stationary AR(1) simulations consequently produced a positive B8-minus-L8 advantage rather than a zero null. Nevertheless, observed primary advantages were substantially larger: sadness 1.174 versus a null mean of 0.322 (95\% null interval 0.040--0.649), stress 3.855 versus 0.649 (0.211--1.119), PHQ-2 0.228 versus 0.044 (0.018--0.073), and depressed mood 0.147 versus 0.020 (0.008--0.033). Each observed value exceeded all but the Monte Carlo correction draw ($p_{\mathrm{MC}}=0.0002$; standardized distance from the null 5.49--20.60). Observed age gradients likewise exceeded their stationary null distributions (all primary $p_{\mathrm{MC}}=0.0002$).

This falsifies the narrow explanation that fitted stationary first-order persistence alone generated the observed magnitude. It does not identify the alternative process: secular trend, study-entry adaptation, context change, higher-order dynamics, non-Gaussian floor effects, or regression after an unusual initial period could all contribute. Earlier permutation and circular-shift analyses were directionally compatible but are reported only in the Supplement because their null assumptions are less appropriate.

In Dejonckheere, inverse-probability weighting for observed responses changed neither primary direction nor inference. The unweighted and weighted slopes were 0.565 versus 0.568 for sadness and 1.496 versus 1.519 for stress in the training-weighted supporting analysis. This sensitivity addresses selection conditional on observed history, not the unobserved values of missed reports.

\subsection{Error reduction survived window and observation checks, but the age gradient weakened after re-anchoring}

The recent-history error advantage was not unique to an eight-report window (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}a). On common targets after report 12, relative MAE changes favoured recent history for each of $K=4,6,8,$ and 12 in the four primary outcomes: sadness ranged from $-9.23\%$ to $-11.21\%$, stress from $-15.71\%$ to $-19.38\%$, PHQ-2 from $-22.82\%$ to $-29.00\%$, and depressed mood from $-23.98\%$ to $-37.89\%$. The full eight-outcome grid was directionally concordant, although the intervals for anger at $K=4$ and 6 narrowly crossed zero (Supplementary Table S13).

EMA timing checks also preserved the principal error contrast (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}c; Supplementary Table S18). B8 spanned only two calendar days in Dejonckheere and three in Marian. After removing day 1 and re-establishing calibration, MAE changes were $-10.14\%$ for sadness, $-18.10\%$ for stress, $-26.47\%$ for depressed mood, and $-25.63\%$ for anhedonia. Adjustment for prompt position gave corresponding changes of $-9.96\%$, $-17.94\%$, $-27.61\%$, and $-29.25\%$. Marian inverse-observation weighting was also directionally unchanged (Supplementary Table S15). Every CES target was at least 14 days after the preceding complete PHQ-2 assessment, so there was no shorter-gap subgroup with which to form a recall-overlap contrast.

Resetting the calibration origin produced a more qualified result (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}b). At late origins, recent history still reduced MAE for stress ($-11.59\%$, 95\% CI $-20.28\%$ to $-2.70\%$), PHQ-2 ($-13.69\%$, $-21.55\%$ to $-5.02\%$), and depressed mood ($-21.42\%$, $-33.99\%$ to $-8.11\%$); the sadness contrast was imprecise ($-7.67\%$, $-20.35\%$ to 6.01\%). By contrast, late-origin age-slope intervals crossed zero for sadness, stress, and PHQ-2, and the anhedonia slope was negative. Middle-origin slopes were also less consistent than original-start slopes. Thus, the error advantage of recent information was more reproducible than the claim that this advantage continually increases with reference age. The original age gradient should be interpreted partly as start-related study position, not as a general decay law.

\subsection{The group pattern was not a universal individual pattern}

Although every mean gradient was positive, only 59.0\%--71.0\% of participants had positive gradients. More importantly, participant-specific gradient ranks did not persist across non-overlapping time blocks. Early--late Pearson correlations were $-0.100$ (95\% CI $-0.369$ to 0.208) for sadness, $-0.084$ ($-0.326$ to 0.150) for stress, $-0.281$ ($-0.454$ to $-0.108$) for PHQ-2, and 0.083 ($-0.183$ to 0.346) for depressed mood. None passed the frozen temporal-generalizability gate. These correlations combine true heterogeneity with substantial estimation error and therefore do not prove that stable individual differences are absent. The negative PHQ-2 correlation may also be accentuated by shared B8 terms and regression to the mean.

By contrast, participants' \emph{mean} update benefit was more stable across blocks: raw-unit Pearson correlations were 0.650 for sadness, 0.496 for stress, 0.748 for PHQ-2, and 0.683 for depressed mood. After division by each participant's within-person standard deviation, correlations remained 0.693, 0.645, 0.657, and 0.793, respectively, with bootstrap intervals above zero. Some people therefore benefited more consistently from recent history, but these data could not reliably estimate a temporally generalizable person-specific age gradient.

Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity} juxtaposes these levels of evidence. Participant-balanced update benefit increased across relative post-calibration time quartiles, but early--late slope correlations remained near or below zero while mean-benefit correlations were positive. Relative quartiles are descriptive positions within each participant's observed sequence, not shared physical-time intervals.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}

Across one sparse multi-year symptom sample and two dense EMA samples, recent personal history predicted later self-reports more accurately than an equally sized early history. Crucially, the empirical advantage was much larger than the advantage generated by participant-matched stationary AR(1) processes. This new null matters because stationarity does not imply exchangeability of distant and recent observations for forecasting: autocorrelation alone already favours recent history. The result therefore supports time-varying relevance beyond fitted first-order persistence, but not a particular psychological mechanism. Re-anchoring preserved late-origin advantages for stress, PHQ-2, and depressed mood but made sadness inconclusive, while age gradients attenuated. The strongest claim is consequently about the comparative usefulness of early and recent histories in these selected samples, not universal baseline decay.

The expanded comparators changed the substantive emphasis. EWM8 improved on L8, but median or modal recent histories often performed still better for bounded, floor-concentrated outcomes. Simple robust summaries also matched or surpassed an online AR(1) predictor. This pattern indicates that both temporal locality and the measurement distribution matter. The study should not be read as proposing a new algorithm; it provides a falsification-based audit of common personal-reference rules.

The equally important negative finding concerns individuality. The group-average age gradient was not shared by everyone, and a person's estimated gradient in one time block did not predict their gradient in another. A baseline can become less useful on average without each person possessing a stable ``decay rate.'' This distinction prevents a group-level property of repeated measurement from being reified as an individual trait.

\subsection{Psychological interpretation}

Personal baselines are often introduced to respect individual differences, but a fixed baseline can become another population-like simplification if it assumes that the earlier person is an adequate reference for the later person. The present findings are consistent with a temporally local view of psychological measurement: personal history is informative, but its meaning is conditional on when it was observed. This view complements work emphasizing that within-person processes cannot be inferred directly from between-person differences and that dynamic conclusions depend on measurement timescale \cite{ref4,ref5,ref6}.

The analyses do not identify why early histories lost relative value. CES spans academic years and the COVID-19 period, whereas the EMA studies span only two or three weeks. Change could reflect symptoms, affect regulation, context, scale use, entry-day adaptation, regression to the mean, or selection into continued responding. First-day exclusion and prompt adjustment make two simple EMA artefacts less sufficient as explanations, and the AR(1) bootstrap makes fitted stationary persistence less sufficient. Neither analysis rules out slow trend, nonlinear dependence, seasonality, or non-Gaussian floor effects. The pseudo-origin results reinforce this caution: error reduction often survived re-anchoring, whereas the age gradient did not. Cross-timescale agreement across three samples argues against one source-specific artefact but does not establish a shared latent mechanism.

\subsection{Implications for repeated assessment and personalisation}

The main practical implication is methodological. Researchers should state whether a personal reference is frozen or updated, report how old its information is at prediction, and compare adaptive rules with simple leakage-safe heuristics suited to the outcome scale. A mean is not automatically the most psychologically faithful summary when scores are ordinal or strongly concentrated at the floor. Likewise, a model can appear personalised because it contains person-specific parameters while relying on an early period that no longer forecasts well. Recent-history prediction and detection of clinically meaningful sustained change remain different tasks; the present study evaluates the former. This interpretation agrees with calls to treat behavioural baselines as task- and context-specific forecasting rules \cite{ref20}.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}

Strengths include the equal eight-report comparison, participant-balanced estimation, transparent evidence chronology, outcome-complete reporting, one outcome-association-blind protocol-frozen external test, a stationary-process falsification model, robust and autoregressive comparators, first-day and prompt-timing checks, and participant-SD standardization. Automated tests verified that current and future reports did not enter predictors and that repeated observations did not masquerade as independent participants.

Several limitations remain. First, two datasets contributed to direction development, only Dejonckheere was outcome-association-blind, and the earlier OSF registration addressed another question. The work is a staged secondary analysis, not a preregistered confirmatory study; the AR(1) null and most robustness analyses are explicitly post hoc. Second, outcomes, scales, reference periods, populations, and schedules differed. This heterogeneity probes a measurement pattern but prevents pooling raw effects or claiming a common disorder process. The multiple outcomes within Dejonckheere and Marian are correlated and cannot be counted as independent replications. Arithmetic means impose interval-like distances on ordinal items; median, mode, and rounded-score results reduce but do not resolve this psychometric limitation. Third, eligibility required repeated completion. IPW addresses only modelled observation processes under conditional missing-at-random assumptions, and CES lacked a comparable scheduled-opportunity denominator. Fourth, the stationary bootstrap inherits an estimated Gaussian AR(1) model. Partial pooling stabilizes short-series coefficients, but model misspecification, trend, nonlinear dynamics, and bounded scores remain plausible. Fifth, age is partly a study-position variable. Pseudo-origins shared observations and depended on retrospectively observed series length, so they are diagnostic rather than prospective validation. Finally, person-specific gradients were imprecisely estimated. Their weak block-to-block correlations cannot distinguish absence of stable heterogeneity from low reliability, and shared B8 construction can induce coupling and regression-to-the-mean artefacts.

Finally, lower prediction error does not establish clinical benefit, diagnostic accuracy, or safety. No treatment decision, deterioration endpoint, or randomized assessment schedule was evaluated. The supplementary deterministic stress exercise is illustrative rather than empirical safety evidence. Prospective event labels and externally specified action thresholds remain necessary before adaptive monitoring can be evaluated clinically.

\subsection{Future research}

A prospective test should freeze early mean, recent mean, EWM, median or mode, online autoregression, and multiple calibration origins before inspecting a new sample. Simulation models should include bounded outcomes, floor inflation, trend, and higher-order dependence in addition to stationary AR(1). Designs with randomized or naturally staggered calibration origins are needed to separate reference age from study-position effects. Person-specific tailoring should be evaluated on new participants and later non-overlapping blocks, with enough repeated observations to estimate gradients reliably. If the intended use is clinical monitoring, short-horizon prediction and externally defined deterioration detection should be evaluated as separate outcomes.

\section{Conclusions}

Across three selected repeated-self-report samples, equally sized recent histories predicted later affect, stress, and depressive-symptom reports more accurately than early histories, by more than fitted stationary AR(1) persistence predicted. The difference survived first-day, prompt-timing, window, and observation-process checks, but the age gradient weakened after alternative calibration origins and could not be estimated as a temporally generalizable person-specific characteristic. Robust recent-history summaries often surpassed the rolling mean for bounded, floor-concentrated outcomes. Personal reference rules should therefore be chosen and audited jointly for temporal location, scale properties, and intended use; these secondary results require prospective confirmation before clinical interpretation.
